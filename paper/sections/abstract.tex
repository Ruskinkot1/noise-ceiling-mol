\begin{abstract}
The same compound measured against the same target in different laboratories often receives different potency values, which limits how well any model can be scored against such labels. For 41 potency tasks (target $\times$ $K_i$ or $\mathrm{IC}_{50}$) from ChEMBL~37 we estimate this limit per task from 8{,}916 molecules reported in at least two source documents, and express model scores as a \emph{fraction of ceiling} $f$ (0: predicting the training mean; 1: reaching the estimated noise floor). The floors range from 0.31 to 0.92 pChEMBL units ($R^2_{\max}$ 0.30 to 0.96). After removing molecules found in MoleculeNet and TDC, we benchmark ECFP-based gradient boosting, random forest and MLP models and frozen ChemBERTa-77M and MoLFormer-XL embeddings with a boosted-tree head under random, scaffold and temporal splits. Frozen embeddings stay below ECFP on every split (Wilcoxon over tasks, Holm-adjusted $p<0.05$), replicating on 24 tasks built from targets that were not in our earlier 17.

The main result concerns the metric rather than the models. ECFP scores below the estimated floor ($f>1$) on 16 of the 41 tasks under a random split, and $f$ rises systematically with the noise it is meant to divide out (Spearman $\rho=+0.74$ against $\hat s$). The cause is how much of a test molecule's own source document the model has already seen: over 609{,}539 single-document test cases, RMSE falls from 1.07 pChEMBL with no same-document training molecule to 0.51 with a hundred or more, crossing a median floor of 0.54. With no such support the three splits give the same error (1.07, 1.08, 1.06), so the familiar ordering random--scaffold--temporal reflects how much document support each leaves rather than a difference in chemical difficulty; a paired test on the same 2{,}096 molecules gives 0.46 with support against 0.91 without. A split that assigns each source document whole puts every task back below the ceiling and removes the confounding with $\hat s$ ($\rho=+0.05$, $p=0.75$), while leaving the model ordering unchanged. Document metadata further shows that replicates are not independent: document pairs sharing an author agree better than pairs that do not ($\hat\sigma$ 0.50 against 0.66 on the first 17 tasks, 0.63 against 0.69 on the 24 added ones), so the floor is if anything conservative. We conclude that the noise ceiling is most useful as a detector of document support rather than as a normalising constant, and release code and data.
\end{abstract}
