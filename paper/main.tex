\begin{abstract}
Molecular property benchmarks are widely used to rank pretrained (``foundation'') chemical models against
classical fingerprint models, yet the labels they use are aggregated from many laboratories and are
known to disagree with each other. We argue that leaderboard differences must be read against the
measurement noise of the labels and against possible overlap between test molecules and pretraining or
benchmark data. We build new, publicly released datasets from ChEMBL in which every molecule is measured
in at least two independent documents and which exclude molecules and documents already present in public benchmarks and in the pretraining corpora of the evaluated models.
From the replicate measurements we estimate a per-task \emph{noise ceiling} on achievable error and
report model quality as a \emph{fraction of the ceiling}. We compare frozen embeddings of foundation chemical models with
ECFP-based QSAR models and simple graph neural networks under random, scaffold and temporal splits, with and without decontamination.

We release the data, code and a leaderboard expressed in fractions of the ceiling.

\section{Introduction}
Benchmarks such as MoleculeNet~\citep{wu2018moleculenet}, the ADMET group of the Therapeutics Data Commons~\citep{huang2021tdc} and
MoleculeACE~\citep{vantilborg2022moleculeace} shape which molecular machine learning methods are considered state of the art.
Three observations make their comparisons hard to interpret. First, the labels are collected from many assays and publications, and
combining IC50 or Ki values from different sources is a documented source of noise: with minimal curation roughly two thirds of points
from different assays differ by more than $0.3$ log units~\citep{landrum2024noise}. Second, a systematic comparison of 25 pretrained
molecular embedding models on 25 datasets found that nearly all of them show negligible or no improvement over the ECFP fingerprint~\citep{praski2025benchmarking}.
Third, audits of public benchmarks report train--test leakage and contradictory labels for identical molecules, and report that such flaws
can change which model ranks first (as summarised in press coverage of an audit of 51 benchmark configurations~\citep{chemistryworld2025audit}; primary source to be added).

Together these observations raise a question the usual leaderboard cannot answer: when a foundation model improves a metric by a few hundredths,
is this a real gain, or is it inside the noise of the labels, or is it caused by overlap with data the model has already seen?
The notion that measurement error caps attainable accuracy is not new~\citep{kramer2012uncertainty,crusius2025fitting}. Crusius et al.\ derive performance bounds for nine datasets by
adding noise of an assumed or published magnitude and find that four of them have been reached or exceeded~\citep{crusius2025fitting}.
We complement this line of work in three ways.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) We estimate the noise ceiling \emph{empirically} from replicate measurements of the same molecule in independent ChEMBL documents,
instead of assuming a noise level. (2) We construct benchmarks from fresh ChEMBL documents that are decontaminated against public benchmarks and the pretraining data of the
evaluated models, and release an uncleaned twin so that leakage can be measured. (3) We propose a ceiling-referenced metric and evaluation
protocol and use it to compare foundation chemical models with fingerprint QSAR models and simple graph neural networks across random, scaffold and temporal splits.

\section{Related Work}

\section{Problem Formalization}
\subsection{Data and noise model}
A \emph{task} $k$ is a pair (target, measurement type), for example (EGFR, $K_i$). Let $\mathcal{M}_k$ be the molecules of the task, and for molecule $i$ let
$D_i$ be the set of documents reporting a value, $n_i=|D_i|$. Repeats inside one document are averaged, giving one value $y_{id}$ per pair $(i,d)$. We assume
\begin{equation}
y_{id} = t_i + e_{id}, \qquad \E[e_{id}]=0,\quad \Var(e_{id})=s_k^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $t_i$ is the molecule's latent value and $e_{id}$ collects between-document variability (laboratory, assay conditions, recording errors). The pooled estimator over molecules with $n_i\ge 2$ is
\begin{equation}
\hat s_k^2=\frac{\sum_i\sum_{d\in D_i}(y_{id}-\bar y_i)^2}{\sum_i (n_i-1)},\qquad \bar y_i=\frac1{n_i}\sum_{d\in D_i} y_{id}.
\end{equation}
This quantity measures \emph{all} between-source variability, including systematic assay shifts, and not only instrument error.

\subsection{Noise ceiling}
Models are trained and evaluated on the document-averaged label $\bar y_i$, whose noise variance is $s_k^2/n_i$.
\begin{proposition}
For any predictor $f$ that does not depend on the test labels, $\E[(\bar y_i - f(x_i))^2]\ge s_k^2/n_i$ for a test molecule $i$, with equality if $f(x_i)=t_i$. Consequently the expected test RMSE over a test set satisfies
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RMSE}_k^{\text{floor}}=\sqrt{s_k^2\,\overline{(1/n_i)}},\qquad
R^2_{\max,k}=1-\frac{s_k^2\,\overline{(1/n_i)}}{\Var(\bar y)} ,
\end{equation}
where $\overline{(1/n_i)}$ is the average over test molecules.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\emph{Sketch.} $\E[(\bar y_i-f)^2]=\E[(t_i-f)^2]+\Var(\bar e_i)\ge s_k^2/n_i$ because $\bar e_i$ is independent of $f(x_i)$. \hfill$\square$

\subsection{Ceiling-referenced metric and leakage}
For a model $m$ with test RMSE$_{k}(m)$ we define the \emph{fraction of the ceiling}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{frac}_k(m)=\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_k^{\text{floor}}}{\mathrm{RMSE}_k(m)}\quad\text{(or } R^2_k(m)/R^2_{\max,k}\text{)},
\end{equation}
and the \emph{leakage} caused by overlap with seen data as $L_k(m)=\mathrm{frac}_k^{\text{raw}}(m)-\mathrm{frac}_k^{\text{clean}}(m)$, where raw and clean denote the undecontaminated and decontaminated test sets. Values $\mathrm{frac}>1$ indicate that a model fits noise or exploits leakage and are flagged for inspection.
Uncertainty is obtained by a cluster bootstrap over molecules.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\begin{description}
\item[H1 (small headroom).] On most tasks the best small model already attains a large fraction of the ceiling; the threshold $\delta_1$ is fixed after a pilot and before the main run.
\item[H2 (differences inside noise).] For most tasks, the paired bootstrap confidence interval of $\Delta_k=\mathrm{RMSE}_k(\text{ECFP})-\mathrm{RMSE}_k(\text{foundation})$ contains $0$.
\item[H3 (fragile advantage).] The gain $G(\text{split})=\mathrm{frac}(\text{foundation})-\mathrm{frac}(\text{ECFP})$ decreases from random to scaffold to temporal splits and after decontamination, and $L_k$ is larger for foundation models.
\item[H4 (diagnostic).] Cases with $\mathrm{frac}>1$ are explained by noise fitting or leakage upon manual inspection.
\end{description}
Any outcome, including refutation, will be reported.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\paragraph{Extraction.} For each target in a list verified by name in ChEMBL we extract $K_i$ and IC$_{50}$ activities with relation ``$=$'', a defined pChEMBL value, empty data-validity comment, binding assay type and target confidence score $\ge 8$, excluding potential duplicates. \TODO{ChEMBL release and extraction date.}
\paragraph{Standardisation.} Largest fragment, neutralisation, canonical SMILES and InChIKey connectivity block (RDKit). Different spellings of the same compound (acid, reordered SMILES, sodium salt) map to one key.
\paragraph{Independence.} The unit of independence is the document; repeated reports of the same assay across documents are collapsed. \TODO{implement and test assay-level collapsing.}
\paragraph{Decontamination.} We build a registry of InChIKeys from public benchmarks and published pretraining corpora of the evaluated models and remove exact matches and near analogues with ECFP4 Tanimoto similarity above a threshold chosen on a pilot (starting value $0.8$). We keep only documents published after each model's training cut-off; where a cut-off is not documented we mark it so. An uncleaned copy is retained.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The ceiling measures between-source variability, not instrument error, and assumes homoscedastic noise and that replicated molecules are typical of the task; replicated molecules tend to be well studied and may be unrepresentative. Documents are the unit of independence but the same assay can appear in several documents. Training cut-offs of some models are undocumented, which weakens the temporal decontamination.
